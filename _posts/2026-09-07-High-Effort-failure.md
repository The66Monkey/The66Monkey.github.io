# Gaming and the games industry

It’s no secret that AAA studios have spent years trying to keep their games out of the hands of Linux gamers. Between Denuvo, firmware‑level anti‑cheat trash, and spyware disguised as “player experience systems,” the message is loud and clear: DON’T PLAY OUR GAMES.

I’ve always thought this was stupid, especially for high‑budget single‑player titles. If you’re making a giant cinematic blockbuster, why wouldn’t you want to sell it to everyone?

But then the best girl on the internet dropped a packed SWO build that runs flawlessly on Linux. It installs cleanly, performs well, and gives you the full Star Wars Outlaws experience.

And that’s exactly why today’s topic is:

# Star Wars Outlaws

Ubislop actually did you a favour by blocking Linux users from playing this thing.

SWO is an insultingly bad game. It’s an idiot savant of failure, managing to screw up every system in the most annoying way possible. There’s something uniquely aggravating about being handed a project that clearly had effort poured into it, yet still lands as completely worthless. It grates against the human spirit to see so much labour wasted on something this hollow.

The core gameplay loop is basically a duct‑walking simulator. Once you’re past the tutorial, half your time is spent squeezing through endless identical ventilation shafts. And let’s be clear: this isn’t stealth. There’s no tension, no risk, no discovery. You hold W, crawl forward, pop out behind an enemy, and then dive into another vent. It’s like the designers built a whole universe out of air ducts and forgot to add a game around them.

Combat exists, technically. You can shoot enemies, you can get into fights, but it’s a soft failure state. Enemies drop nothing. You gain nothing. You don’t progress. All combat does is slow down your walk from mission start to the terminal you need to hack. It’s busywork disguised as gameplay.

SWO somehow ends up worse than the sum of its parts. The nerf‑gun combat is functional enough, the open world technically exists, the game runs, characters speak lines. Yet the whole thing is painful to play. It’s like every system is individually mediocre, but when combined they form a black hole of boredom.

I’ve played other Ubislop titles like AC:Odyssey and GR:Breakpoint. Odyssey was fine — janky, grindy, but good enough to carry me through the story. Breakpoint, once you strip out gear score, scratches the “shoot some dudes and clear a base” itch.

SWO doesn’t scratch anything. The designers somehow managed to strip out every reason to interact with the game’s systems. Combat gives you nothing. Stealth is a slog. Loot floods you with junk that never matters. The economy pretends to exist, but every upgrade is locked behind some quest‑specific “rare” item, which makes all the credits and materials you collect feel like background noise rather than progression.

The whole loop collapses under that emptiness. You can fight, you can loot, you can scrounge — none of it moves the needle. If the game let you save in hostile areas, you could delete the combat entirely and the experience wouldn’t change. All that would remain is the one thing SWO actually leans on: stealth that somehow manages to be even less engaging than the shooting.

## The Characters, or Whatever You’d Call This Cast

Kas starts out fine for about a minute. There’s this flicker where you think she might grow into the role, maybe bounce off the world, maybe show some edge. Then it all falls apart instantly. She’s bland in a way that feels manufactured — like someone scrubbed her down until nothing interesting was left.

The bigger problem is she has nobody around her who can lift the material. No rival pushing her, no partner sharpening her, no mentor challenging her, no antagonist forcing her to react. She just drifts through scenes, talking into empty space. Every conversation feels like placeholder dialogue that never got replaced. You can practically hear the writers straining for emotional beats, but they never give Kas anything to work with.

And the worst part is that this exact fantasy has already been done well. SWTOR’s Imperial Agent storyline nailed the vibe of a morally flexible operator navigating the Star Wars underworld. That was an MMO — the genre Ubislop has been flirting with in their single‑player games for years. They had a working template sitting right there. All they had to do was adapt it into a focused solo experience. Instead they managed to fumble the easiest shot imaginable.

It’s the same curse that sank Skull & Bones. They had the pirate magic from Black Flag ready to be refined, and somehow turned it into a monument of wasted potential. SWO feels like the spiritual sequel to that disaster — a studio staring directly at a winning formula and still steering straight into a ditch.

Kas could’ve been a great character. The story could’ve carried her. The cast could’ve lifted the world. Instead everything feels like it was stitched together from scraps of other games, none of which fit. It’s a parade of missed opportunities marching across a galaxy that deserved better.

What fries my brain is how those scraps got downgraded. Kas isn’t just underwritten — she’s stuck inside systems that actively drag her down. Every time the game tries to give her momentum, one of its broken mechanics yanks her straight back into the mud.

The combat feels like someone took Breakpoint, scooped out everything that made firefights satisfying, and replaced it with toy‑gun nonsense. The exploration somehow lands below Odyssey, which is wild because Odyssey already had its own share of repetition. SWO doesn’t just borrow from those games — it copies the wrong parts, waters them down, and then acts shocked when the whole thing collapses.

Every system feels like it was designed by a different team that never spoke to the others. You can imagine the meetings: “We have this mechanic from Game A, this idea from Game B, this half‑finished concept from Game C — let’s glue them together and hope nobody notices.” And instead of landing at “mediocre but functional,” the whole thing sinks below the baseline of every game it cannibalized.

That’s why the “idiot savant of failure” line fits. SWO doesn’t just miss the mark — it invents new ways to screw up things that were already solved problems. You’d think copying working systems from your own catalogue would be the easiest job in the world. Yet somehow they managed to downgrade everything they touched.

## The Promise vs. The Reality

SWO had the kind of pitch that practically writes its own hype. A scrappy outlaw clawing their way into the Star Wars underworld, stealing a ship, blasting through space, taking jobs, dodging syndicates, and carving out a place in the galaxy. Add some simplified Freelancer‑style space combat and you’ve got the sort of concept people have been begging for since the early 2000s.

And somehow, despite delivering those ingredients on paper, the final product is still awful.

It’s almost impressive how thoroughly SWO wastes its own premise. You’d think a game built around being a rising scoundrel would lean into improvisation, chaos, and clever escapes. Instead you get a checklist simulator where every “high‑stakes” moment feels like it was assembled by a committee terrified of letting the player have fun.

And then there’s the story.

I Noped‑the‑fuck‑out the moment I realized the entire overarching plot is just “assemble a crew to redo the exact same heist you already did in the intro.” Who the hell thought this was exciting? “Can we beat the guy we already beat? Can we break into the same vault again?” Wow. Incredible stakes. Truly breathtaking narrative ambition. This could maybe work as mission two, but as the grand finale? Holy shit, what a failure.

It’s like the writers took the opening mission, slapped a piece of tape on it that says “finale,” and called it a day. The whole thing feels like a pitch meeting where someone said, “What if we just… do it again?” and everyone nodded because they wanted to go home.

SWO is the kind of game that looks incredible in trailers, screenshots, and curated review builds. The moment you actually play it, the illusion collapses. Every system fights every other system. Every mechanic feels like it was designed in isolation. Every moment that should be exciting is smothered under layers of pointless busywork.

It’s a game that technically exists, technically functions, technically delivers its promised features — and still manages to be a miserable slog.
