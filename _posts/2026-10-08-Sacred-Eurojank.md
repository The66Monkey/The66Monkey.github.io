# Sacred Gold

It has taken some time for my new GPU to arrive, so I ended up digging through older games that can happily run on integrated graphics. That led me back to Sacred Gold, a game title i remember from back in the days, but don't think I ever played.

The experience was strange because a few weeks ago I had revisited Diablo II: Resurrected and come away far less impressed than I expected.

The atmosphere is still excellent. Blizzard's art direction remains strong, the soundtrack hit's right in the nostalgia, and there are moments that instantly transport you back to the early 2000s. Yet actually playing it left me surprisingly cold. The story takes a long time to get going, the pacing often feels sluggish, it was a very "gameplay" focused game, which made it feel amazing at the time, but now more clearly showes it age.

Sacred Gold is very different. The animations are "old school". The writing is rougher. Large parts of the game feel as though they were designed by a team that really wanted to do a certian "type of game".

What struck me almost immediately was how differently it approaches the idea of an RPG world.

Most modern ARPGs are built around efficiency. The map exists to deliver content. The player moves from objective to objective along routes that are only one step away from a corridor shooter. Every area has a reward (and it usually is singular), and every minute is expected to contribute to progression.

Sacred comes from a very different school of thought.

The world often feels as though it was built first and populated with quests afterward. Roads wander across enormous stretches of countryside. Villages appear in places that don't seem particularly important. Entire sections of the map exist without any obvious justification beyond the fact that somebody thought they would be nice to have there.

It carries much more of the pen-and-paper RPG world that someone just made up. And it so nice to not have something that feels like every other focus tested game.

A great many locations don't matter, many sidequests are almost gags in how simple and inconsequential they are. You can run halfway across the world and discover little more than a side quest and a merchant. The reward is exploration and discovery, paced lightly by level gating.

That may sound like faint praise, but I think something has genuinely been lost as games have become more focused on optimization. Designers have become increasingly uncomfortable with allowing players to simply exist in a world. Everything must provide a curve adjusted amount of perfect reward and scaling.

Another choice that slaps you in the face like a trout is the way enemies eventually stop being hostile. As your character grows stronger, most creatures cease attacking altogether and become neutral.

It took me a little while to realize that previously hostile armies of thieves where totally unconcerned with my presence, because it feels so completely at odds with contemporary design. We've spent the last decade watching developers invent increasingly elaborate scaling systems to ensure that every enemy remains relevant forever. Sacred solves the problem by simply deciding that some enemies no longer matter.

It's not realistic and it isn't especially elegant, but it is this alternative solution to a problem we just don't see today. Imagine in Skyrim if bandits in the starting area just stopped worrying about you after a few hours, instead of the highwayman robbing peasants for carrots all of a sudden upgrading his fur armour and rusty sword to a full set of ebony armour and mastercrafted elven swords.

The world is just another tool, and it really smells of engineers building worlds, instead of dedicated storytellers.

You are not fighting mathematically adjusted wolves for the next forty hours. Those wolves have been left behind. Regions that once felt threatening become places you can casually ride through and just marvel att the absurd monster density. There are thousands of theives gathered around a tiny little farm in the middle of nowhere.

I don't think modern developers would ever make that decision.

The more time I spent with Sacred, the more it reminded me of the games Majuular discusses when covering Ultima and the early PC RPG scene. It shares their sense of ambition. There is a persistent feeling that the developers wanted to create an entire fantasy world and bolted on whatever systems needed to make a game in it.

Modern ARPGs are often far better designed than Sacred. They have smoother progression, stronger balance, and a clearer understanding of what players actually enjoy doing.

Unfortunately they also tend to feel remarkably similar to one another, and Grim Dawn is maybe the sadest example. It still lives on my steam deck, but man is that game so damn bland in many aspects, it does nothing inasne or out there. Perfectly fine in every aspect, but way more forgetable.

Sacred feels like the product of people experimenting, Grim Dawn is following a recipie.

Looking back, Sacred feels less like a forgotten classic and more like an evolutionary branch that never became dominant. Diablo's interpretation of the genre ultimately won. The modern ARPG inherited its focus on efficiency, progression loops, endgame systems, seasons and optimization. Sacred points toward a different future that never arrived, one where the genre borrowed more from sprawling RPG worlds than from dungeon crawlers.

I still bemoan that Massive World Role Playing Games never became a thing, we gave the huge worlds with the casual grind to the Online space, and gave the offline players the corridors.

Sacred remains fascinating because it preserves that alternative path, and i do hope more modern game designers look back at what could have been.

I'm not going to finish it. The story hasn't grabbed me and there isn't enough momentum pushing me toward the ending. Yet I'm glad I revisited it.

The genre moved on, the industry chose a different direction, and Sacred was largely left behind. Twenty years later, that may be the most valuable thing about it. It allows you to look backward and see all the roads that weren't taken.

Also this game has a mounts, they work really well, and the fact that more modern games has not stolen the design and dared to make their worlds bigger is baffling.

Make big, interesting words for us to explore, go a little crazy.
It's more fun that way.
