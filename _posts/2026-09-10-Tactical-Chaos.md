# Tactical Shooters: Almost good

There’s a tiny genre that has always been close to my heart.
No battle passes, no cinematic cutscenes every ten minutes, no “live service roadmap.” Just slow, methodical room‑clearing where one bad angle ends the whole run.

The geanera had it's golden days in the late 90's early 00's, Rogue Spear was the first one where it really clicked for me, and in many ways the genera peaked and died with SWAT 4.
These days the genera is niche, the ARMA series is probably the highest profile series that serves the mainstream, excellent simulation, but not very fun games. So you’re left with small teams, weird engines, broken AI, and games that look like they were assembled in a garage. And somehow, that’s where some of the best tension in gaming still lives.

I went through three of them:

    Zero Hour

    Ready or Not

    Black One Blood Brothers

They all aim for tactical realism. They all miss in different ways. And one of them accidentally gets closer to SWAT 4 than it has any right to.

## Zero Hour — the barebones breach

Zero Hour is tiny, Short campaign. Mid graphics. No story. UI held together with tape.

But once you’re actually inside a building, gun up, leaning around a doorframe, it suddenly becomes something sharp.

The pacing is slow, but the levels are small and the rooms are tight. So the atmoshpere stays tight without dragging.
Enemies aren’t geniuses, but they’re placed in ways that both allows for taticall solutions while punishing overly aggresive play.

And then there’s the K9 system — a feature that should elevate the game but ends up fighting against it.

A dog in a CQB game is a brilliant idea. It adds a chaotic, urgent element that isn’t just another grenade or gadget. When the dog charges into a room, you’re committed. You have seconds to back it up before things go sideways. It’s one of the few mechanics in the genre that creates tension dynamically, without relying on a bomb timer or scripted pressure. It changes the rhythm of a breach in a way that feels genuinely fresh.

But the implementation is completely misaligned with the rest of Zero Hour.

If your entire SWAT team gets wiped, they’re magically ready for the next mission.
If the dog dies, you’re locked out of using it for multiple missions.

This creates a backwards incentive structure. Losing operators — the thing that should matter — barely affects anything. Losing the dog — the thing that should be a dramatic moment — just encourages you to restart immediately. There’s no long‑term progression, no XP, no unlocks, no campaign persistence. So the cooldown doesn’t protect anything meaningful. It just punishes you for engaging with one of the game’s most interesting mechanics.

Instead of adding tension, it undermines it.
Instead of creating stakes, it creates avoidance.

It feels like a feature designed by someone with a completely different vision for the game — someone who thought Zero Hour needed a “cost” for using the dog, without considering how players actually interact with the rest of the systems. The result is a mechanic that should make the game more exciting, but instead pushes you toward save‑scumming and resets.

Zero Hour has one more design issue that drags missions down: objective items are almost impossible to see. Whenever you need to grab a folder or interact with a laptop, the game suddenly turns into a search‑and‑rescue operation for props. There’s no UI hint, no outline, and under NV they blend into the environment completely. It’s common enough that players have built maps of spawn points just to avoid running in circles.

A subtle indicator wouldn’t break immersion. It would simply make the objectives readable in a game where the tension comes from clearing rooms, not scanning desks for a texture that looks identical to the background. Zero Hour’s encounters are strong when they’re about angles and pacing; they fall apart the moment you’re forced to hunt for an item that doesn’t want to be found.

Zero Hour is stripped down to the bone. There’s almost nothing extra. No elaborate progression, no sprawling customization, no narrative wrapper. It’s just: here’s a building, here are suspects, here’s your team, don’t screw it up.

And because it focuses so hard on that one thing, it ends up feeling more like SWAT 4 than anything else on the market — even with the K9 system tripping over its own feet.

## Ready or Not — the beautiful structural failure

Ready or Not is the opposite problem of Zero Hour.

On paper, it’s the “better” game.  
Better command system.  
More levels.  
Clearer visuals.  
AI teammates that actually act without babysitting.  
More customization.  
Unlockable cosmetics as rewards.  
More narrative and environmental storytelling.

You boot it up and it looks like the future of tactical shooters. Then you start playing, and something feels wrong in your bones.
The surface problems are obvious:
The compliance system feels disconnected from the rest of the game. Civilians and suspects don’t react in ways that build tension — they behave like they’re following two completely different rule sets. Sometimes they stand still waiting for instructions, other times they bolt the moment they see you, and the game never establishes a rhythm you can read or anticipate. It’s not unpredictability in a good way; it’s unpredictability that feels unintentional, like the AI is flipping between behaviours that were never tuned to work together.

But the deeper rot is architectural.
Ready or Not feels like a game built by people who understand the *components* of tactical shooters but not the *hierarchy* of them. The levels are filled with AI meshes, clutter, props, and visual noise. There’s a ton of stuff in your peripheral vision. The game is busy. It looks lived‑in.

What it doesn’t have is a clear set of deliberate elements that define the encounter.

You don’t need a room stuffed with assets. You need a room that communicates danger, sightlines, and intent. You need spaces that create tension through layout, not decoration. Ready or Not often feels like a diorama where the important parts are buried under noise.

The result is a game that has all the scaffolding of a great tactical shooter — systems, commands, AI routines, level dressing — but the actual experience never crystallizes. You’re going through the motions, but the emotional weight isn’t there. The tension doesn’t build. The payoff doesn’t hit.

And then you reach the ending — or rather, the absence of one.

If you finish the final mission successfully, you get nothing. No cutscene. No text crawl. No wrap‑up. You’re dumped back at the station like you just finished a random mid‑campaign level. It feels like the game forgot to acknowledge that you completed its entire narrative arc.

But if you make the mistake of pressing **F** on the detonator — the same button you’ve used to secure every other piece of evidence in the entire game — you don’t secure it. You *activate* it. And suddenly the game plays a tiny “ending” scene that feels like it was hidden behind a practical joke.

It’s a perfect snapshot of Ready or Not’s design problem: the game is full of systems, assets, and ideas, but the connective tissue — the part that makes those pieces feel intentional — is missing. The structure is there, but the payoff isn’t. The game keeps building toward something, and when you finally reach the top, there’s nothing waiting for you.

Ready or Not tries to be everything at once, and the core gets smothered under its own ambition.

## Black One Blood Brothers — the ambitious papercraft

Black One Blood Brothers sits in a completely different lane, it’s chasing the old Rainbow Six and Rogue Spear DNA.

The tactical map is the strongest part of the game. You’re not just breaching a door; you’re building an entire operation. You draw routes, assign teams, sync timings, decide who covers what, and then watch the plan either come together or collapse. It’s the only game of the three that actually lets you design a mission instead of just reacting to one.

But everything wrapped around that core is uneven.

The interface is barebones.
On‑the‑fly commands are nowhere near Ready or Not’s.
Gunfeel varies heavily between weapons—some feel sharp, others feel like placeholders.
The levels are big and sprawling, but a few missions feel more like proof‑of‑concepts than finished scenarios.
And despite years in Early Access (since January 2022), parts of the game still feel like prototypes taped together.

The biggest red flag is feature removal.
Female operators and K9 units were cut because the developer wasn’t happy with their implementation. I get that it’s Early Access, but removing entire systems instead of simplifying or reworking them is a worrying sign. It makes you wonder what the release version will actually look like—and what else might disappear along the way.

The Prisoner of War mode is the clearest example of how good ideas get stranded.
On paper, it’s brilliant: a tactical “escape” scenario where you start with nothing and fight your way out by looting gear. In practice, the design undercuts itself. Corpses aren’t persistent in a way that supports reliable scavenging, the looting interface is clumsy, and you can’t take armor or clothing from enemies. Because armor lives in the customization layer rather than the loot system, you’re stuck grabbing guns and a few gear items while stuck in t-shirt and jeans. That’s fine in the normal game; in PoW, it guts the whole concept.
It also highlights how something strange, you can customize your guns endlessly, but the enemies all use one of 4 or 5 guns, one for each class, with no differencisation in mods. If this mode didn't exist you might never have noticed, but again this "good idea" mode accidentally shows you how undercooked the whole system is.

Black One Blood Brothers is full of these moments: strong ideas, rough execution. Every part shows promise, but the game needs to stop stacking experiments and start finishing modes with a clear vision. If it doesn’t, it risks staying in permanent “almost there” territory.

It’s the most replayable of the three because the planning layer always generates new problems.
It’s also the one most at risk of never fully solidifying.

A tactical sandbox with huge potential—still made of paper.

# Which one is best?

None of these games are bad, but they all land in strange places.

Zero Hour and Ready or Not are both “two‑week games.” They’re worth picking up on a Steam sale, you’ll have a good time clearing rooms, and then you’ll probably never touch them again. Zero Hour nails the moment‑to‑moment tension but has almost no structure around it. Ready or Not has structure everywhere but never quite gets the moment‑to‑moment right. They each succeed at the part the other struggles with, and neither becomes something you return to long‑term.

Black One Blood Brothers is different. The planning layer could be the thing that sets it apart — if it were smoother, faster, and easier to use. Right now it’s clunky, slow to work with, and often gets in its own way. The potential is there, but the current iteration doesn’t fully deliver on it. After years in Early Access and multiple features being removed outright, it’s hard to believe the game will reach the state it’s aiming for. At the moment it’s more of a study piece: a collection of strong ideas wrapped in thin execution. Interesting to explore, but not something you recommend without explaining the caveats first.
We just need a game that can actually pull all of it together — a modern title with a clear vision, finished systems, and the confidence to commit to what makes this genre special. Hopefully someone will eventually deliver the masterpiece
