# Rexxar the Barbarian

After more than twenty years, I still remember Rexxar's journey in *The Founding of Durotar* more clearly than most RPGs I have played since.

That is a strange achievement for a Warcraft III bonus campaign. The characters are archetypes with just enough personality to make them memorable, the story is straightforward, and the entire thing can be completed in a handful of evenings. Yet whenever I replay *The Founding of Durotar*, I am reminded how many larger RPGs still struggle to create the same sense of adventure.

What I love is that Rexxar is exceptional, but not special.

The campaign begins with a dying messenger asking him to deliver a message. Rexxar accepts responsibility for it because it is the right thing to do. That single decision draws him deeper into the affairs of the Horde. One obligation leads to another, one problem reveals the next, and before long he is dealing with demons, political tensions and threats to the future of an entire people.

The adventure feels earned because it grows naturally.

Rexxar keeps helping, keeps travelling and keeps accepting responsibilities that would be easy to ignore. The story never needs to convince the player that the next destination matters because every destination follows from the last one. The road through the campaign feels less like a sequence of quests and more like a chain of consequences.

The closest comparison is probably Conan.

Conan rarely sets out to save kingdoms or defeat ancient evils. He takes a job, follows a rumour, helps someone in trouble or chases the prospect of wealth. The story expands around him. What begins as a practical concern slowly turns into something much larger. His importance comes from his determination, courage and willingness to act when others hesitate. Rexxar operates on much the same principle.

Both characters are exceptional. They are stronger, tougher and more capable than most people around them. At the same time they remain relatable because their defining traits are recognizably human. Persistence is understandable. Loyalty is understandable. Standing by your friends is understandable. Most people can picture themselves trying to live up to those ideals.

The companions play an equally important role. Rokhan (best boy, fight me!), Cairne and Misha never feel like gameplay systems attached to the protagonist. They feel like fellow travellers. Every addition to the group makes the adventure feel larger. The journey becomes a story about relationships, alliances and mutual trust as much as demons or politics.

I suspect this is part of the reason *Dragon Age: Origins* remains so memorable as well. The companions represent connections to different factions, interests and perspectives. Gathering allies feels meaningful because every new relationship broadens the world and changes how the player understands it.

This became much more obvious when I recently returned to World of Warcraft. More specifically, I sat down with version 3.3.5a, a notepad and tried to figure out why I was never truly captured by it despite loving so much of the setting.

The early Horde experience captures some of the same energy. Razor Hill feels like a frontier fort. The Crossroads feels like an outpost holding together an enormous wilderness. The Horde feels young and uncertain. The relationship between the orcs, trolls and tauren creates the sense that something is being built.

For a while, it feels like you are participating in the same project that Rexxar helped create.

The Barrens remains one of my favourite zones in any MMO because it functions almost like a frontier novel. Settlements are small, vulnerable and practical. The people living there have immediate concerns. Trade routes need protecting. Harpies are causing problems. Centaurs are raiding. The world feels connected because every problem appears to belong to the same larger story.

As the game opens up, that momentum gradually fades. The world becomes larger and more impressive, but the journey becomes increasingly fragmented. The player travels through fascinating places filled with interesting stories, yet many of those stories feel detached from one another. My quest log filled up with tasks that somebody clearly thought were important, but the thread connecting them became harder and harder to follow.

By the time I reached Gadgetzan, and saw once again one of the most memorable locations in Warcraft, the Caverns of Time, I was flooded with nostalgia. It is my favorite location in the game, but after that rush of nostalgia, the post-nut clarity hit hard!
It should be the beginning of the final act, the point where the adventure should suddenly widen in scope and reveal what everything has been building towards. By that point I had crossed continents, mastered the elements as a shaman, fought my way through the dangers of the frontier, and arrived at a wound in reality itself. Time travel, ancient mysteries and the foundations of the Warcraft setting were sitting right in front of me.

The game treats the location as little more than another stop on the leveling journey. Rather than gathering momentum, the adventure seems to lose it. Instead of opening the next chapter, it points you towards more quest hubs, more levels and more errands. The world continues to get more interesting while the player's journey becomes impossible to care about.

That contrast kept bringing me back to *The Founding of Durotar*.

The campaign understands that progress is not the same thing as adventure. Progress is experience points, levels and better equipment. Adventure comes from the feeling that the next step matters because of everything that came before it. It comes from travelling with people whose company you enjoy. It comes from wondering what waits over the next hill while still remembering why you started walking in the first place.

At a glance, WoW looks like it might be the same thing stretched out. You still start by helping people with local problems and eventually find yourself dealing with existential threats. Yet somewhere along the way the connective tissue begins to weaken. Other players do not solve this problem either. I can have fun with friends watching paint dry.

Modern RPGs provide bigger worlds, deeper systems and more content. Games have always competed on scale. Bigger maps, more classes, longer quest logs, larger lore libraries and increasingly elaborate progression systems. Yet here we are, surrounded by supposedly endless entertainment, and somehow I remember travelling across Kalimdor with a troll, a tauren and a bear more clearly than most of it.

I appreciate stories built around capable people rather than chosen people. Whether it is Rexxar, Conan, the crew of Star Trek, or even Yahtzee Croshaw's largely forgotten *Mogworld*, the stories that stay with me tend to revolve around people solving problems because they feel responsible for them. Competence, loyalty, friendship and the willingness to keep moving forward turn out to be far more interesting qualities than destiny.
